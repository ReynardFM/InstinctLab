Instinct Lab: how it was built

A small world where creatures with tiny brains evolve. No one tells them what to do: they are scored, the best have children, and over time they get better at staying alive. This report walks through the project one step at a time, from a single brain to a living food chain. Each finished chapter runs the real simulation in your browser.

Chapter 1

One creature's brain

Each creature has a tiny neural network. Every moment it reads four numbers about the world: where the nearest food is (left or right, ahead or behind, how far) and how much energy it has left. Six middle neurons mix those numbers. Two outputs decide what it does: how much to turn and how fast to move.

The brain is just 44 numbers: the strength of each connection. A creature is born with them and never changes them. Nobody writes them by hand. At the start they are random, so creatures wander around blindly.

Like a car with a steering wheel and a pedal, driven by a dice roll. Evolution keeps the drivers whose dice happened to point at food.

40 creatures (green) and food (yellow beads). The ringed one is the best eater right now; its brain is on the right.

Blue = positive, red = negative. Thicker lines are stronger connections. A filled circle is a neuron firing.

Each generation, every creature plays three food maps. The ones that ate the most become parents. Their children get the same 44 numbers with small random changes (mutations). Press "Evolve" and watch the line climb: after a few dozen generations the creatures turn toward food on their own.

Chapter 8

Poison up the food chain

Now the world runs on its own, with no generations: prey eat plants and have babies when they are full; hunters catch prey and have babies too. Some plants are poisonous. A prey that eats one gets sick: everything costs it more energy, and most die soon after. A hunter that catches a sick prey takes in all of its poison.

Can hunters learn to leave sick prey alone? Below are two copies of the same world, from the same start. The only difference: on the left, hunters see a glow on sick prey. On the right, they are blind to it.

prey sick prey glow (only the left hunters see it) hunter plant poison plant

Glow: hunters can see who is sick

Blind: hunters can't tell

Speed

"Pick" measures how choosy hunters are. It is the share of sick prey among their catches, divided by the share of sick prey alive. 1 means they catch sick prey exactly as often as chance. Below 1 means they avoid them. It needs a few thousand ticks to settle.

What we found, over 14 different worlds of 200,000 ticks each:

Measured November 8, 2026 (fast hunters, full poison). In a longer test (6 worlds, 400,000 ticks, twice the food) the gap kept growing: 0.43 against 0.57 in the last stretch. The two live worlds above are just one of those 14, so their numbers can differ a lot from these averages; that is why every result here uses 14 worlds.

So the hunters with glow learned to dodge. But they did not end up more numerous. A better hunter eats more of the healthy prey, so there are fewer prey left, so hunters go hungry again. The gain shows up as more hunters for each prey, not as more hunters.

Like a fisherman who learns to skip the bad fish. He catches more good ones, the lake empties faster, and next year he eats about the same as before.

This puzzle drove the next chapters: why doesn't avoiding poison pay off, and what would nature do?
